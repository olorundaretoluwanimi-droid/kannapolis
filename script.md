# Script — What Happened to Kannapolis?

Narration (N) in Storyteller Oxley. [Cards] are on-screen quote cards, read in the voice only where marked.

## Cold open — 30 July 2003

N1. Kannapolis, North Carolina. Wednesday, the thirtieth of July, two thousand and three.
N2. That morning, the company that ran the mills here stopped. Four thousand, three hundred and forty people lost their jobs on the same day. It was the largest single layoff in the history of the state.
N3. For most of the century before, this town had no mayor, no council and no city hall. It had a company. The company owned the mill, the houses, the streets, and, people said, the weather.
N4. Three years later, the mill itself came down.
N5. This is the story of the town that made America's towels. Who built it, who worked in it, and what happened when the looms stopped.

## I · The Towel

N6. It begins seven miles to the south, in Concord. In eighteen eighty-seven, a merchant named James William Cannon organised a cotton mill there.
N7. Cannon had been selling cloth for years. He noticed something. Farm families bought cloth by the yard, and the cheapest, simplest thing a family could not make at home was a towel.
N8. In eighteen ninety-eight, his mills switched from plain cloth to towels. They were the first towels ever made in the South.
N9. Towels sold. So Cannon needed a bigger mill, and a place to put the people who would run it.
N10. In nineteen-oh-six, he bought about eight hundred acres of farmland, on the old wagon road between Salisbury and Charlotte.
N11. On those fields he built a mill, and around the mill, a town. He asked the county to give it a name, starting with a K.
N12. Kannapolis. You will often hear that it means "city of looms." Local historians are not so sure. The likeliest meaning is the plainest one: Cannon's town.
N13. A mill town in the South was a world of its own. The mill needed hands, and the hands came from the farms, whole families at a time.
N14. The machines were the same everywhere. Cotton went in at one end, as bales. It was opened, cleaned, combed into ribbons, drawn out, twisted into thread on spinning frames, wound on bobbins, and woven on looms.
N15. In Kannapolis, almost all of it became one thing. Towels. And later, sheets.
N16. By nineteen fourteen, Cannon was making more towels and sheets than any company in the world.

## II · The Children

N17. In nineteen twelve, a photographer came to Kannapolis with a heavy camera and a notebook. His name was Lewis Hine.
N18. Hine worked for the National Child Labor Committee. His job was to prove, with pictures, what children were doing in American factories.
N19. He wrote down what the children told him. Their names, their ages, how long they had worked.
N20. This is Herman Parker. Hine's caption says: "said six years old, and he and others said he had worked in the Cannon Mills, Kannapolis."
N21. Hine gave the picture a title of his own. "The logical conclusion of the laissez-faire policy."
N22. Outside the public school in Kannapolis, he photographed a group of children. Every one of them, he wrote, said he or she had worked in the cotton mills. The two smallest boys said they were ten.
N23. In Concord, at the Cannon mill there, he met a boy whose mother could not spell his name. Hine wrote it down as Otie Honeycott. Otie said he had been doffing for two years.
N24. Doffing meant pulling the full bobbins off the spinning frames and putting empty ones on. It was quick work, for small hands.
N25. And Hine photographed the houses. "Some of the housing conditions of the workers," he wrote, "to contrast with the homes and gardens sometimes shown from the show mills of the state."
N26. North Carolina's own laws allowed much of what he saw. It would take a federal law, the Fair Labor Standards Act of nineteen thirty-eight, to keep children out of the mills for good.
N27. We do not know what became of Herman Parker. But we know what one child of the Cannon mills remembered, twenty-six years later.

## III · The Company Town

N28. In nineteen twenty-one, James Cannon died. His youngest son, Charles, took over. He was twenty-eight.
N29. In nineteen twenty-eight, Charles Cannon brought nine of the family's companies together into one: Cannon Mills Company.
N30. Under him, Kannapolis grew into one of the largest unincorporated towns in America. There was no city government. The company was the government.
N31. The fire insurance maps show it. Here is Kannapolis in nineteen twenty-six. The mill, the railroad, and rows of small identical houses, numbered like parts.
N32. And here it is in nineteen forty-three. The same streets, filled in. Plant Number One has spread across the map, and the houses run out in every direction.
N33. The company built them, owned them, and rented them to the people who worked in the mill. It painted them, repaired them, and decided who could live in them.
N34. It built the YMCA, the churches, the stores. Workers called the boss Mister Charlie.
N35. In September nineteen thirty-eight, a writer for the government's Federal Writers' Project sat on a front porch in Concord with a spinner named Alice Caudle.
N36. Alice was forty-seven. She had worked for the Cannon Mills for more than thirty years.
N37. She told the writer how it began.
Q1 (card + read). "I was so little I had to stand on a box to reach my work."
N38. She was ten. She learned spinning, then spooling, then the new winding machines. She was good at it, and she knew it.
Q2 (card + read). "If I'd a-been a man no telling how far I'd-a gone."
N39. In nineteen thirty-eight, her shift ran from seven in the morning to half past three. For two full weeks of work she was paid thirty-one dollars. The rent on a company house was six dollars a month.
N40. That spring, the company held a supper in Kannapolis for everyone who had worked twenty-five years or more. A car came to fetch her. There was turkey, and white tablecloths from the company's own bleachery.
N41. Charles Cannon made a speech, and gave out pins. Alice remembered what he said.
Q3 (card + read). "He told about the way young'uns used to stand on boxes to work, the way I done."
N42. When the office first called her in about it, she thought she was going to be fired. When they handed her the ticket, she had a question.
Q4 (card + read). "Are you right sure they're not a-going to take hit off my pay?"
N43. And at the end, sitting on the porch, she said the thing she wanted written down.
Q5 (card + read). "As long as I can work and talk and laugh, I'm happy."
N44. In August nineteen forty-two, the First Lady came. Eleanor Roosevelt asked to see what Cannon Mills provided for the people who worked there, and toured the YMCA in Kannapolis.
N45. By then the town's directories ran to hundreds of pages. Name after name, and beside so many of them, the same three words. Cannon Mills Company.

## IV · The Vote

N46. A company town is a quiet town, until it isn't.
N47. In September nineteen thirty-four, textile workers across the South walked out in the largest strike the industry had seen. Mills were picketed. In some places, troops were sent in.
N48. In Kannapolis, the union never got a foothold. Not in the thirties, and not for decades after.
N49. In nineteen seventy-four, Cannon workers voted on a union for the first time. The union lost.
N50. In nineteen eighty-five, they voted again. It lost again.
N51. In nineteen ninety-one, and in nineteen ninety-seven, there were more votes. Both times, federal labour officials threw the results out, finding that the company had intimidated union supporters.
N52. Then, in June nineteen ninety-nine, after twenty-five years of trying, the workers of the Kannapolis mills voted the union in. Around five thousand of them.
N53. It was called the greatest union victory in the history of the southern textile industry.
N54. It came very late.

## V · Sold

N55. Charles Cannon died in April nineteen seventy-one. For fifty years, he had run the mills, and the town, like a household.
N56. Without him, the company was a large business like any other, and large businesses can be bought.
N57. In nineteen eighty-two, a California investor named David Murdock bought Cannon Mills, with borrowed money.
N58. Two years later, in nineteen eighty-four, Kannapolis finally became a city, with a mayor and a council of its own. It had been, until then, the largest unincorporated community in the United States.
N59. Murdock soon sold the mills on, to Fieldcrest. The workers' pension plan was closed, and its money moved into annuities with an insurance company. When that insurer later failed, retired mill workers saw their pensions cut, by around a third.
N60. Fieldcrest Cannon was bought in turn, in nineteen ninety-seven, by a Texas company called Pillowtex.
N61. Meanwhile, towels were being made more cheaply abroad. Year by year, more of the towels and sheets on American shelves came from other countries.
N62. In two thousand, Pillowtex went bankrupt. It came out of bankruptcy in two thousand and two. It did not last.

## VI · The Last Shift

N63. On the thirtieth of July, two thousand and three, Pillowtex ran out of money, and stopped.
N64. In Kannapolis and the counties around it, four thousand, three hundred and forty people lost their jobs.
N65. Some had worked there for thirty or forty years. Some were the grandchildren of people who had worked there. Some were the grandchildren of children like Herman Parker.
N66. One dot for every job.
N67. That was the day the town that made America's towels made its last one.

## VII · What Stands There Now

N68. Plant Number One was enormous. Nearly six million square feet. About the size of the Pentagon.
N69. In two thousand and five, the wreckers came. In two thousand and six, the last buildings and smokestacks were brought down with explosives.
N70. The buyer of the land was the same man who had bought the company in nineteen eighty-two. David Murdock.
N71. Where the mill stood, he built a research campus, for the science of food and health.
N72. The old street names are still there. West Avenue. Main Street. Some of the mill houses still stand, painted now, with gardens.
N73. And the town has a council, a mayor, and a city hall. The things it waited eighty years to have.
N74. So what happened to Kannapolis?
N75. A family built a town around a towel. For almost a century, the town gave the mill its children, its parents, its whole working life. When the mill went, the town stayed.
N76. Herman Parker was six. Alice Caudle was ten. The last workers walked out in two thousand and three.
N77. The looms are gone. The town is still here.
